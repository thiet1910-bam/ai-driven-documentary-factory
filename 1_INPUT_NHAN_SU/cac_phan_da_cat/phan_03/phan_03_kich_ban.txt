Two Fridays in a row. And then America forgot about it. For 17 years, Alaska had no real government at all. Then in 1896,  someone found gold. Not in Alaska, just across the border in Canada's Klondike.

But the fastest way in was through Alaska. A 100,000 people dropped everything and headed north. To cross the border, each person had to carry a full year of supplies, about a ton. So, they climbed a frozen mountain pass in a single line, 1,500 steps carved into the ice, up and down 30 or 40 times.

Most never found gold. The people who got rich were the ones selling the shovels. One saloon owner in the town of Gnome was a retired law man you might have heard of.  Surprised? Wyatt Herp? Because by then gold had turned up in Alaska itself, in Juno, in Fairbanks,  and in Gnome, where it was lying right on the beach.

The ice box was full of treasure.  In the winter of 1925, Alaska gave the world a different kind of story. In the town of Gnome, children began to die of dtheria. The medicine was almost 700 m away.

No road, no plane that could fly, 50° below zero.  So 20 men and 150 sled dogs ran it in a relay through a blizzard in 5 and 1/2 days. The town was saved. A dog named  Balto ran the final stretch and became world famous.

There's a statue of him in New York Central Park. But the real hero was a dog named Togo  who ran more than 250 m, almost five times farther. Sorry, Baldo. Then came the war. In June 1942, Japan attacked Alaska.

Its troops landed on two islands, Atu and Kiska, and  held them for about a year. It was the only time in the Second World War that enemy soldiers occupied American soil in  North America. Taking Atu back cost hundreds of American lives  in one of the hardest battles of the Pacific.

Then came Kiska. 34,000 soldiers stormed the island and found nobody there. The Japanese had slipped away two weeks earlier, hidden by the fog. Suddenly,  Washington understood how exposed Alaska was.

In just 8 months, American soldiers built a highway 1,700 m long through pure wilderness  to connect it to the rest of the country. Alaska was no longer forgotten. In 1959, it  became the 49th state.
